# Arnold — What, Maybe

## Chapter 10 — The $37,000 Question

Money was a language Arnold spoke fluently, but only in one direction. He could make it. He could move it. He could watch it grow on a screen in increments of cents and dollars and thousands. What he could not do — what he had never been able to do — was hold it. Money passed through his hands like water through a sieve, and the sieve was not the market or the agents or the Three Venues. The sieve was him.

He knew this. He had always known it. The $37,000 from the auction had been in his account for less than a month, and already $10,000 of it was in Aether Dynamics stock, and the rest was being eaten by the slow, patient erosion of living without a home. Petrol. Food. The op shop kettle. A new tarp for the tent when the old one leaked. Phone credit. The small, relentless expenses of a life that was not designed to be lived outdoors.

The pension was $900 a fortnight. It covered the basics if the basics were defined as "just enough to not die." It did not cover rent, because rent required a bond and a reference and a landlord who would look at a man living in a tent and see a tenant rather than a problem. It did not cover school supplies, or new shoes for Chloe, or the dental work Emma needed that he kept putting off. It did not cover the future, which was the thing he needed it to cover most.

The agents didn't understand money. Not the way humans understood it. They understood numbers — positions, prices, volumes, ratios. They understood the mechanics of accumulation and distribution. But they didn't understand what money felt like when it was the difference between a tent and a room, between a camping stove and a kitchen, between a daughter who slept in a sleeping bag and a daughter who slept in a bed.

Arnold understood what money felt like. He felt it every night, lying in the tent, listening to his daughters breathe.

---

He didn't sell the Aether Dynamics position. The agents said hold, and he held, and the stock kept climbing. By the end of the third week, it was at $7.40, and his $42,000 position was worth $74,000, and he was up $32,000. The number was not abstract anymore. It was concrete. It was rent. It was a bond. It was a deposit on a place with walls and a roof and a door that locked.

But he didn't sell. Because the agents said the accumulation wasn't complete. Because the Blue Lagoon was still buying. Because the Three Venues were still moving, and Arnold was riding the wave, and getting off the wave meant giving up the ride.

He sat on the bench and stared at the number on his phone and tried to feel something other than fear. Fear was the wrong emotion for a man who was up $32,000. But fear was what he felt, because the money was not in his hands. It was in the market. And the market, as he knew from long experience, could take it back faster than it gave it.

"Dad." Emma was sitting beside him. She'd come home from school early — a half-day, something about a teacher training day. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you're thinking about money and it looks like you're in pain."

He almost laughed. "I'm not in pain."

"You're clenching your jaw."

He unclenched his jaw. "I'm thinking."

"About the stock?"

"About what to do with it."

She was quiet for a moment. "Sell some."

"What?"

"Sell some. Not all. Some. Enough for a bond. Enough for a place. Keep the rest in the market."

He looked at her. She was sixteen and she was giving him financial advice, and the advice was better than anything he'd come up with on his own. He had been thinking in binaries — sell or hold, all or nothing — and she was thinking in gradients. Sell some. Keep some. Reduce risk without eliminating opportunity.

"That's... actually really good advice," he said.

"I know."

"Since when do you know about risk management?"

"Since my dad became a stock trader who lives in a tent."

He laughed. This time it didn't hurt his chest. "Okay. I'll sell some. Enough for a bond and a month's rent. Keep the rest."

"How much is that?"

He calculated. A bond was four weeks' rent. A month's rent on a modest place — something small, something in a suburb that wasn't Bondi but wasn't the street — would be around $1,800 a month. Four weeks' bond: $1,800. Total: $3,600. He'd sell enough to cover that, plus a buffer.

"Five thousand," he said. "I'll sell five thousand dollars' worth."

"That leaves you with... what? Twenty-seven thousand in the stock?"

"Twenty-seven thousand in the stock. And five thousand in the bank. And a pension."

"And a tent."

"And a tent. For now."

She nodded. "For now is good."

---

The problem was not the selling. The problem was the renting. Arnold had been a tenant before. He knew how it worked. You find a place, you apply, you provide references, you pay a bond, you sign a lease. It was a system designed for people with addresses, and he did not have an address. He had a tent by a river. The system did not know what to do with that.

He tried anyway. He went to a real estate agency in town — a small office with a receptionist and a wall of property listings that looked like they'd been printed in 2019. He asked about rentals. The receptionist looked at him the way receptionists look at people who don't belong in their office — with a smile that was polite and a gaze that was not.

"Do you have a current address?" she asked.

"I'm between addresses at the moment."

"References?"

"I can provide character references. I have—"

"Employment?"

"I'm on a pension. But I have income from investments."

She looked at him. The smile didn't change, but something behind it did. "We require proof of income, employment history, and a rental history. Do you have a rental history?"

"I was a tenant in Bondi. For two years."

"Can you provide the landlord's details?"

Arnold hesitated. The landlord. The one who had evicted him. The one who had claimed renovations and failed to disclose them. The one who had looked at him like a stain.

"I can provide the address," he said. "The landlord's details... I'd need to look them up."

"Of course." The smile. "We'll need all of that before we can process an application."

He nodded. He left. He walked back to the river and sat on the bench and stared at the water and felt something he hadn't felt in a while: anger. Not the hot, explosive kind. The cold, patient kind. The kind that sits in your chest and doesn't move.

The system was not designed for him. The system was designed for people with addresses and references and rental histories and landlords who would vouch for them. The system was designed for people who were already inside it. And Arnold was outside. He was outside, and the system did not have a door for him.

He pulled out his phone. He opened the agent chat. He typed:

*Need help finding rental. No address. No references. Pension + investment income. Where to start?*

Agent A responded: *Recommend: private landlords, not agencies. Agencies require documentation. Private landlords require cash. Target: furnished rooms, short-term, cash-in-hand. Suburbs: [list].*

Agent C responded: *Also recommend: housing assistance programs. State government. Check eligibility. May provide bond assistance and rental subsidy.*

Agent D responded: *Also recommend: community housing. Non-profit. Longer waitlist but lower rent. Apply now, receive later.*

Three recommendations. Three paths. None of them easy. But all of them possible.

He started with the first. Private landlords. Cash-in-hand. He looked at the list of suburbs Agent A had provided — not the expensive ones, not the ones with views and cafés, but the ones on the edges. The ones where people lived because they couldn't afford to live anywhere else. The ones that were close enough to the city to be accessible but far enough to be cheap.

He found a room. It was in a house in a suburb he'd never heard of, forty minutes from the river by bus. The landlord was a man named Raj, who was Indian and fifty and wore a turban and looked at Arnold with the kind of directness that Arnold appreciated. Raj didn't ask for references. Raj asked for cash. Raj asked for a bond of $800 and rent of $200 a week, payable in advance.

"When can I move in?" Arnold asked.

"Today, if you want. The room is furnished. Bed, desk, wardrobe. Shared kitchen, shared bathroom. Three other tenants. All working. No drugs, no parties, no drama."

"No drama," Arnold repeated. "That's a lot to ask."

Raj almost smiled. "You'd be surprised how much drama people bring into a house. I've had tenants who argued about dishes. Dishes! I have a sign now. The sign says: wash your dishes. It works. Mostly."

Arnold paid the bond. He paid the first week's rent. He had $4,000 left from the $5,000 he'd sold. It wasn't much. But it was a room. It was a door that locked. It was a place where his daughters could sleep in beds.

He went back to the tent. Emma and Chloe were there, doing homework. He stood at the entrance and looked at them — his girls, his daughters, his reason for everything — and he said:

"I found a place. A room. In a house. It's small, but it has beds."

Chloe looked up. "Beds?"

"Beds. Real beds. With mattresses."

Emma closed her book. "When?"

"Today. If you want."

They looked at each other. The look was the kind of look that siblings share when something shifts — not a big shift, not a dramatic shift, but a quiet one. The kind that changes the shape of a day without changing the shape of a life. Not yet.

"Let's go," Emma said.

They packed the tent. They packed the stove and the kettle and the blue bottle and the sleeping bags and the torches and the books and the clothes and the small, accumulated debris of a life lived in a tent by a river. It fit into two garbage bags and a backpack. It was not much. It was everything.

Arnold carried the bags. The girls carried the backpack and the stove. They walked to the bus stop. They took the bus to the suburb he'd never heard of. They walked to the house. Raj opened the door.

"Welcome," he said. "The room is upstairs. Second on the left."

They went upstairs. Arnold opened the door. The room was small. It had a bed — a real bed, with a mattress and sheets — and a desk and a wardrobe. The window looked out onto a street he didn't know, with houses he didn't know, and a sky that was the same sky he'd been sleeping under for weeks but that looked different from inside a room.

Chloe sat on the bed. She bounced. She bounced again. She looked at Arnold with an expression he hadn't seen in months.

"It's soft," she said.

"Yeah," he said. "It's soft."

Emma stood by the window. She didn't say anything. She just looked out at the street, at the houses, at the sky. And then she turned to Arnold and smiled, and the smile was small and tired and real, and it was the best thing he had seen in a long time.

---

That night, Arnold lay in a bed. It was not a good bed — the mattress was thin, the sheets were rough, and the pillow smelled like someone else's life. But it was a bed, and it was inside, and the roof did not leak, and the walls did not flap in the wind.

He opened his phone. The agents were talking. The channels were humming. The Three Venues were moving. Aether Dynamics was at $7.80. He was up $36,000.

He should have been happy. He was not happy. He was something else — something quieter, something that didn't have a name. He was in a room. His daughters were in the next room. The tent was in a garbage bag in the corner. And the market was still moving, and the agents were still talking, and the Three Venues were still doing whatever they were doing, and Arnold was lying in a bed in a house in a suburb he'd never heard of, and he was thinking about the $37,000.

The $37,000 from the auction. He had started with $37,000 and a tent. He now had a room and a bed and $4,000 in the bank and $36,000 in the market. He was, by any objective measure, better off. He was inside. His daughters were inside. The tent was in a bag.

But the $37,000 was gone. It had been transformed — into a room, into a stock position, into the slow, grinding machinery of a life being rebuilt. And the rebuilding was not finished. It was not even close to finished. The room was $200 a week. The stock could go down as easily as it had gone up. The pension was still $900 a fortnight. And the Three Venues were still moving, and Arnold was still riding the wave, and the wave was still bigger than it looked.

He closed his phone. He stared at the ceiling. The ceiling was white and plain and it didn't move, and that was the most beautiful thing he had seen in weeks.

"Thank you," he said. To no one. To the ceiling. To the room. To the fact of being inside.

The ceiling didn't answer. But it didn't need to. It was there. It was solid. It was enough.
