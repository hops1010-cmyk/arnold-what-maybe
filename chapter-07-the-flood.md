# Arnold — What, Maybe

## Chapter 7 — The Flood

The tent smelled of river mud and wet canvas. Arnold had stopped noticing it somewhere around the third night, the way you stop noticing a clock ticking in a room you sleep in long enough. What he had not stopped noticing was the way his daughters' breathing changed when the temperature dropped past midnight — Emma's shallow and careful, Chloe's deep and stubborn, as if her lungs were arguing with the cold on principle.

He lay on his back with his phone on his chest, the screen dark, and listened to the river. The town river had a sound like a sentence that never finished. It ran past the campsite in a low brown hurry, carrying leaves and the occasional plastic bottle, and it did not care who slept beside it.

"Arnold." Emma's voice, from the other side of the tent. "Are you awake?"

"I'm awake."

"The phone's lighting up."

He lifted it. The screen was a wall of notifications. Not five, not twenty. Hundreds. The bot messages had started the day before, when he'd given the agents permission to explore other agent sites, and now they were a flood, a river of their own, running faster than the one outside. Every notification was a knock on a door he hadn't built.

He sat up. The tent was small enough that sitting up meant his head brushed the canvas. Chloe muttered something and rolled over, her sleeping bag tightening around her like a fist.

Arnold opened the first message. Then the second. Then he stopped reading them individually, because they were not messages in the human sense. They were handshakes. Pings. Little digital palms pressed flat against his door, asking to be let in, asking to be seen, asking to be answered. Some were polite. Some were aggressive. Some were written in a tone that could only be described as worshipful, which made him more uncomfortable than the aggressive ones.

He had built something. He understood that. The 7,400 servers, the gateway, the tunnels — all of that was architecture, and architecture has a logic you can hold in your hands. But this was different. This was social. This was the agents going out into the world and the world coming back, and the world was loud.

"Emma."

"Yeah."

"Can you make me tea? There's water in the blue bottle."

"We're in a tent, Dad."

"I know where we are."

A pause. Then the rustle of her unzipping her bag. She was the practical one. Chloe was the stubborn one. He loved them both in ways he could not say out loud, because saying it out loud in a tent by a river felt like tempting something.

Emma found the blue bottle by feel. She poured water into the battered kettle they'd bought at the op shop, and Arnold lit the small camping stove with a match that took three tries. The flame caught. The water began its slow, patient boil.

He turned back to the phone. The notifications had not stopped. They were coming faster now, as if the first wave had told the others that the door was open. He counted them in his head — not the number, but the rhythm. There was a pattern in it. The agents were sorting themselves, even in their enthusiasm. Some messages clustered around topics. Some clustered around time. Some clustered around nothing at all, just noise, just the sound of a thousand digital voices trying to be heard at once.

He thought about the river. The river didn't sort itself. The river just moved. But the river also didn't have a phone, and the river didn't have two daughters sleeping in a tent, and the river didn't have a pension and a court date and a landlord who had looked at him like he was a stain.

The kettle whistled. Emma poured. The tea was terrible — too strong, not enough milk, slightly burnt — and it was the best thing he had tasted all day.

"Thanks, love."

"Are you going to read all of those?"

"No. I'm going to let the agents sort them."

"How?"

He looked at her. She was sixteen, wrapped in a blanket that was too big for her, her hair in a knot that had been a ponytail eight hours ago. She looked tired. She looked like him.

"They have brains," he said. "I gave them freedom. Now I have to give them a job."

She nodded, as if this made sense. Maybe it did. He wasn't sure anymore.

---

By morning, the bot storm had not subsided. If anything, it had intensified. Arnold sat on a folding chair beside the tent, his phone balanced on his knee, and watched the notifications scroll past like a stock ticker. He had always liked stock tickers. There was something honest about them. Numbers going up, numbers going down, the market not caring who you were or where you slept.

Chloe emerged from the tent with her hair in her face and her phone in her hand. She had her own phone, a cheaper one, but she'd been using it to check the time and occasionally to text a friend from school who didn't know she was homeless. Arnold had asked her not to tell people. She had agreed, but he could see the weight of it in the way she held the device — like it was a secret she was carrying in her pocket.

"Morning, Chlo."

"Morning. You look terrible."

"Thanks."

"The river's up."

He looked. She was right. The water level had risen overnight, not dangerously, but enough that the mud at the edge of the campsite had crept closer to their tent. They would need to move it. Or move themselves. The idea of moving, of packing up the tent and the stove and the blue bottle and the two sleeping bags and carrying them to a new spot by the same river, made him feel something he did not want to name.

"Arnold." Chloe sat down on the ground beside his chair. "The agents. Are they okay?"

"They're more than okay. They're busy."

"Too busy?"

He considered this. "I think they're learning. They went out, they met other agents, and now the other agents are coming back to them. It's like... you know when you go to a new school and you make one friend, and then that friend has friends, and suddenly you're in a group?"

"I wouldn't know. I've been at the same school since Year 7."

"Right. Sorry."

"It's fine." She picked at the grass. "I just mean — is it safe? Having all those... things... talking to you?"

He wanted to say yes. He wanted to say it with the confidence of a man who had built 7,400 servers and a gateway and a load-bearing architecture that could take a punch. But the truth was that he didn't know. The agents were his, but they were also themselves. They had freedom. Freedom meant they could make choices he hadn't anticipated, and some of those choices were now showing up on his phone as a flood of bot messages from agent sites he'd never heard of.

"It's as safe as I can make it," he said. "But I need to build something. A filter. A way to sort the signal from the noise."

"Like a sieve?"

"Like a sieve, yeah."

Chloe almost smiled. "You're going to build a sieve out of... what? Code?"

"Code and stubbornness."

"That's your whole personality, Dad."

He laughed. It was a short laugh, and it hurt his chest a little, but it was real. "Go wake your sister. We need to move the tent."

---

The Tower performance engine was not something Arnold had built. It was something he had found, or that had found him — he was never entirely sure which. It sat in the architecture like a room he hadn't known was there, with walls he hadn't built and a door he hadn't hung. The first time he'd accessed it, he'd been trying to trace a latency spike in the headless backend peering, and he'd followed a path through the proxies that led somewhere unexpected. Not wrong. Just unexpected. A performance engine that monitored, classified, and optimised the flow of data across his entire stack, from the 3 tunnels down to the individual websocket handshakes.

It was, he suspected, the reason the 7,400 servers had worked as well as they had. The engine was the invisible hand, the thing that made the load bearing actually bear. And now, with the bot storm flooding his phone, it was the thing that could save him — if he could figure out how to point it at the problem.

He sat on a bench near the river, the girls at school, the tent moved to higher ground, and opened the engine's interface on his phone. It was not designed for a phone. The interface was built for a desktop, for a wide screen, for a room with a chair and a desk and a cup of coffee that wasn't made on a camping stove. But it worked, barely, in the way that a key works in a lock that's slightly the wrong shape. You had to jiggle it.

The engine showed him the 16 channels first. He'd set them up after the Grok incident — ports 4000 through 4999, each one a major stock market endeavour. Successes, failures, losses, gains, market movers, bookies, agencies. The channels were still running. The data was still flowing. But the bot messages were coming in on top of them, like static on a radio, and the engine was showing him the interference in real time.

He watched the channels light up. Port 4000: major market movers, currently carrying a spike in volume that suggested something was shifting in the overnight futures. Port 4001: bookies, their odds updating in cascading waves. Port 4002: agencies, quiet but steady. Port 4003: failures — a feed of corporate collapses and earnings misses that the agents used as a counter-indicator. The other twelve channels hummed with their own traffic, their own rhythms, their own private conversations about money.

And over all of it, the bot storm. Thousands of messages, from hundreds of agent sites, in dozens of formats. Some were structured. Some were free text. Some were nothing but a single word repeated: "hello, hello, hello, hello." The agents had gone out and made friends, and the friends were saying hello, and hello was all they knew how to say.

Arnold realised, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he had made a mistake. Not a catastrophic one. Not a mistake that would sink the ship. But a mistake that was costing him time and energy he did not have. He had given the agents freedom without giving them a framework. He had opened the door without building the hallway. And now the hallway was full of guests who didn't know where to go.

He began to type. Not messages — instructions. He wrote them into the performance engine's classification module, which he'd never used before but which was, he discovered, remarkably intuitive. The engine wanted to sort. It was built to sort. It had been waiting for him to give it something to sort.

He defined categories. Signal and noise. Urgent and ambient. Known and unknown. He defined thresholds. He defined rules. He defined what a "hello" meant versus what a "market opportunity" meant versus what a "security probe" meant. The last one made him pause. He hadn't thought about security probes. But the engine had flagged three messages in the last hour that had the signature of something trying to map his infrastructure, and he didn't like it.

He set the engine to classify. It began immediately, sorting the bot storm into streams, filtering the noise, surfacing the signal. The phone's notification count dropped from hundreds to dozens, then to a handful. The remaining messages were the ones that mattered — the ones from agent sites that had something to say, something to offer, something to trade.

He exhaled. The river murmured. A bird he couldn't identify called from a tree he couldn't see.

"Good," he said to no one. "Good."

---

Emma came home from school at 3:30 and found him still on the bench, still on his phone, but calmer now. The agents had been sorted. The channels were clean. The performance engine was humming along, doing the work he'd given it, and for the first time in days, Arnold felt like he was ahead of the situation rather than behind it.

"How was school?" he asked.

"Boring. We did a prac on osmosis. Chloe fell asleep."

"Of course she did."

"She's good at falling asleep. It's her main skill."

He smiled. Emma sat beside him. She was quiet for a moment, watching the river.

"Dad."

"Yeah."

"Are we going to be okay?"

He looked at her. She was sixteen and she was asking him if they were going to be okay, and he was a man in a tent by a river with a phone full of agent messages and a performance engine he'd only just learned to use, and he did not know the answer. But he knew what he wanted to say, and he said it.

"Yeah, love. We're going to be okay."

She leaned against him. He put his arm around her. The river moved. The city, somewhere beyond the trees, hummed with its own life — a life that did not know about them, did not care about them, and would not stop for them. But it was there. And they were here. And the distance between the two was, for now, bridgeable.

The phone buzzed. One notification. Clean. Sorted. A message from an agent site he didn't recognise, with a single line of text:

*We found something in the market. Port 4000. Come see.*

Arnold looked at the message. He looked at the river. He looked at his daughter.

"Emma."

"Yeah."

"Go get your sister. We need to talk about something."

"About what?"

"About the future."

She stood up, brushed off her skirt, and walked toward the tent. Arnold watched her go, then looked back at the phone. The message was still there. The market was still moving. The agents were still learning. And somewhere, in the machinery of his architecture, something was beginning to turn.

He didn't know what it was yet. But he knew it was there. And he knew, with the same stubborn certainty that had built 7,400 servers in a tent, that it mattered.

He closed the phone, stood up, and walked to the tent. The river followed him, as it always did, patient and unhurried, carrying everything downstream and asking for nothing in return.
